Budget proposal · Partner training programme

A self-service Xill4 sandbox for partner training

Give every partner engineer their own Xill4 instance and their own practice data, and let the exercises mark themselves. Xill4 already ships as a Docker image, so this is cheaper than it sounds.

PREPARED BY KAMIL BEELI DE BELFORT DECISION REQUESTED: BUDGET APPROVAL STATUS: PHASE 0 COMPLETE

The ask

Approve a phased budget, released one phase at a time

What is being requested

Approval to build a multi-tenant Xill4 training sandbox in four phases, with funds released per phase and a go/no-go at each boundary.

Phase 0 is already complete at no cost. Phase 1 is the only immediate spend — a working single-trainee sandbox and a pilot — and it produces the measurements that make Phase 2's cost exact rather than estimated.

The recurring cost this replaces is 24 to 40 hours of senior consultant time per cohort — three to five working days — spent supervising trainees, answering the same questions, and marking migrations by hand, including the practical exam.

That is not an occasional cost. One trainer alone delivered six cohorts in the first nine months of 2026, and there are two more active partner trainers, with a fourth currently being trained.

Fifteen cohorts is a deliberate floor: it assumes the two other trainers each deliver fewer than the six one trainer has already delivered in 2026. Senior time is costed at €800 a day, or €100 an hour.

For scale: at roughly €10,000 to deliver one partner training, fifteen cohorts is about €150,000 a year of programme cost — of which supervising and marking is a quarter to two-fifths. That is the portion this proposal addresses.

Note what the fourth trainer represents: the current way to deliver more training is to train more trainers. This proposal is the alternative to that — capacity that grows without adding senior headcount, and delivery that stays identical whoever is running the cohort.

The strategic case is ramp-up. Partner engineers currently take more than two months to become genuinely project-ready; the target is four weeks. Every week earlier a partner becomes productive is a week Xillio is not absorbing work the partner was contracted to do.

The problem

Three costs, all of them recurring

Senior time spent watching and marking

A trainer sits with a cohort, answers the same questions, then reviews each trainee's migration by hand to judge whether it is correct — the practical exam included. Between 24 and 40 hours per cohort, across at least fifteen cohorts a year and three trainers.

It consumes exactly the people who are most valuable on client work, and the only way it currently scales is by training another trainer — which is itself senior time, and introduces a second source of judgement about what counts as a correct migration.

Trainees cannot practise at the same time

Partner training servers take one or two logins, and concurrent work on a single Xill4 instance is not advisable — trainees interfere with each other. So people queue, or they watch instead of doing. Hands-on practice is the entire point of the training, and it is the thing the current setup rations.

Ramp-up takes more than two months

Competence at the end of training is already assessed: there is a practical Xill4 exam in which an engineer performs a simple test migration. The gap is not assessment — it is everything before it. Practice is rationed, feedback arrives days after the work, and the exam is a single gate at the end rather than a signal a trainee can act on while learning. Someone also has to mark it by hand.

The sandbox does not replace that exam. It feeds it, by giving trainees unlimited graded practice beforehand — and the exam itself becomes one more scenario, marked automatically like the rest.

End goal

What partner training looks like when this exists

A partner engineer opens a browser, signs in, and gets their own Xill4 instance with their own copy of a realistic messy dataset. They read the brief, build the migration in Xill4, and press Check my work. Seconds later they see a score, which tasks passed, and exactly which values were wrong.

They fix and re-check as often as they like. When they pass, the next scenario unlocks. The trainer never marks anything by hand, and sees a dashboard of who has passed what, how many attempts it took, and which exercise the cohort is getting stuck on — which is the signal that says the material needs work, not the trainee.

Because each trainee's practice data is generated from their own identity, no two trainees have the same answer, and an answer passed between them scores nothing.

Ideally the partner also picks the systems they want to practise against — a file share into SharePoint Online, OpenText Content Server into SharePoint, and so on — so the practice matches the migration they have actually been contracted to deliver. The section below sets out how that works and what each system costs to add.

Why now

Xill4 ships as a Docker image, which is what makes this affordable

This was the open question that decided whether the idea was cheap or expensive, and the answer is favourable. Xill4 publishes an official container image, exposes a single port, and is configured entirely through environment variables — a licence key, a MongoDB connection string, and an environment secret.

That means a trainee's sandbox is a container, not a virtual machine. A dozen of them share one host, each pointed at its own database on one shared MongoDB, and one can be reset in seconds rather than redeployed in minutes.

Systems

Which systems trainees practise against, and how partners choose

The sandbox is a real Xill4 instance, so it can do anything Xill4 can do: crawl a file share, load into SharePoint Online through the Migration API, extract from OpenText Content Server. What differs between systems is not whether it works, but what each one costs to isolate per trainee.

One asymmetry keeps this affordable

A source is read; a target is written. So a whole cohort can share one source system read-only, while every trainee needs their own target space. Twelve trainees extracting from the same OpenText instance do not interfere with each other. Twelve trainees writing into the same SharePoint site absolutely would.

One shared target site, a folder per trainee

The cheapest way to give thirty trainees a SharePoint Online target is not thirty site collections. It is one target site with a folder per trainee: nothing to provision per person, one place to configure, and a reset is deleting the contents of one folder.

The obvious objection is that trainees can see each other's folders and copy the answer. That objection is already answered by the way practice data is generated: each trainee's source data is different, so a migration copied from a colleague's folder scores zero. Visibility stops being a threat. Where discretion still matters, SharePoint can give a folder unique permissions, which is reasonable at cohort scale.

The one thing worth measuring rather than assuming is concurrency. Throttling limits apply at tenant and site level, so a dozen simultaneous migrations into a single site may throttle where the same load spread across several sites would not. Start with one site and folders; split into separate libraries or sites only if measurement says to. Phase 1 is where that gets measured.

How partners get to choose: adapters, not more exercises

Offering a menu of source and target combinations sounds like it multiplies the work — five sources times five targets would be twenty-five exercises to write and mark. It does not, because an exercise's learning objective is separable from the systems it runs against. “Normalise inconsistent metadata and load it without losing records” is the same lesson whether the target is a file share or SharePoint.

So each system needs one adapter, built once: something that creates a trainee's own space in it, seeds it if it is a source, and checks what landed in it if it is a target. Every existing exercise then works against every adapter. Adding SharePoint Online as a target makes it available to the whole curriculum, not to one exercise.

That turns the menu into a realistic sequence: start with file share and object storage, which are instant and free; add SharePoint Online next, because it is what partners overwhelmingly migrate into; add legacy sources like OpenText as the licensed instances and the demand appear.

Two honest limits

A real cloud target is slower and less predictable than a stand-in, and the consequence lands on the trainee. A SharePoint Online migration job is submitted, queued and completed asynchronously: if the grader checks the moment the trainee presses the button, it can see half the documents and report a correct migration as incomplete. If five trainees push at once and the service throttles, the same thing happens more often. If Microsoft has a bad afternoon, nobody in the cohort can pass.

So a real target obliges the grader to poll rather than assert, to distinguish “not finished yet” from “finished wrongly”, and to tell a trainee plainly when a failure was the platform's rather than theirs. All solvable, none free — whereas a local file share or object-storage stand-in answers instantly and identically every single time.

And a licensed legacy source has to exist somewhere to be shared. If Xillio has an OpenText instance for internal testing, pointing the cohort at it read-only is straightforward; if not, that becomes a cost line of its own and the alternative is practising on a pre-extracted dataset, which teaches transformation and loading but not extraction.

Evidence

The hard part is already built and proven

Automatic marking is where projects like this usually fail. It has therefore been built first, before any spend, and it works.

A complete exercise exists today: a legacy client file share with a hand-maintained metadata export containing three date formats, an uncontrolled department column, records pointing at files that are absent, and files nobody documented. The trainee must normalise it, load it into a target structure, and report what could not be migrated.

The marking engine scores that work automatically, out of 100, and names the specific values that are wrong — for example expected 2016-04-03, actual 04/03/2019. It marks the outcome, never the method, so there is more than one valid way to solve an exercise.

Payback

The addressable cost today is €36,000 to €60,000 a year. The sandbox does not remove all of it — a trainer still runs the cohort and still answers questions. What goes to essentially zero is marking, and what falls is supervision, because trainees get an answer in seconds instead of queuing for the only person who can give them one.

Assume conservatively that half is recovered: €18,000 to €30,000 a year, against a one-off build of €16,000 to €26,400. That pays back within the first year, and from the second year on it is recovery rather than investment.

Two things improve that arithmetic and are not counted in it. The fourth trainer's cohorts add to the saving instead of adding cost. And partners reaching project-readiness in four weeks instead of two months is worth considerably more than the marking hours — it is simply harder to put a defensible number on, so it has been left out.

Risks

What could go wrong, and what it would cost

One Xill4 container turns out to be resource-hungry

Fewer sandboxes fit per host, so Phase 2's hosting cost rises. This is precisely what Phase 1 measures, before the larger commitment is made.

Writing exercises is the real ongoing cost, not infrastructure

Each scenario needs its data generator and its marking rules — a day or two of skilled time. Infrastructure is cheap; curriculum is the investment. Better to plan for it than to discover it.

Trainee work lives in MongoDB, not on disk

The container image stores projects and flows in the database. Resetting or preserving a trainee's work is therefore a database operation, which needs designing properly in Phase 1 rather than assumed.

Exercises that pass but teach the wrong thing

A marking scheme can be technically correct and pedagogically wrong. The pilot exists to catch that with a real person, and every exercise ships with checks proving it both passes correct work and fails incorrect work.

Real systems can fail a trainee who did everything right

Migrations into a live service complete asynchronously and throttle under concurrent load, so a grader that checks too early sees an incomplete result and marks correct work as wrong. Nothing erodes trust in automated marking faster. The mitigation is to use deterministic stand-ins for the exercises that teach technique, keep real services for the exercises where a real service's friction is itself the lesson, and make the grader wait and retry rather than assert.

Alternatives considered

Commercial hands-on training platforms exist that provide sandboxes and automated challenge checks. They would remove the provisioning and dashboard work, but we would still write every exercise and every marking rule ourselves — the part only Xillio can do — and we would take on a recurring licence fee and a dependency on their support for a container-based product. Given that Xill4 already ships as a Docker image, building is the cheaper and more controllable route. Worth revisiting if Phase 2 proves harder than expected.

Decision

What approval unlocks immediately

With Phase 1 approved, work starts the same week: pull the image, stand up one sandbox with its database, seed it, and put a colleague through the existing exercise in real Xill4. That produces three things a decision on Phase 2 needs — a working sandbox, measured cost per trainee, and a real person's verdict on whether the marking is fair.

The recommendation is to approve Phases 1 and 2 with a ceiling, release Phase 1 now, and hold Phase 2 until Phase 1 reports.
